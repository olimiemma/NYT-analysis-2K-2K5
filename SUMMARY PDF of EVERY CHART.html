This is an exploratory data-engineering project examining how New York Times headlines changed from 2000 through 2025 across 2.2 Million Headlines.
Revealing shifts in attention, defining eras, and the changing tone of the news. 

 

The analysis covers publishing volume, recurring vocabulary, era-defining terms, lexical sentiment, named entities, and a semantic-search workflow.
Let's get straight into it.

1. Did the News Get Darker?

Sentiment dropped sharply after September 11th and never fully recovered to its 2000 baseline. It brightened modestly around Obama’s 2008 election. And from 2017 it entered a long decline that reached the lowest point of the entire quarter century in 2025.

2. How much did The Times actually publish, year over year?

Volume climbed through the 2000s, peaked in 2006 at over 163,000 articles, then declined through the 2010s. By 2022 the dataset shows fewer than 20,000 articles for the entire year, before recovering into 2023–2025.

Is that decline real? Partly. Some of it reflects a genuine industry shift: newsrooms shrank, and The Times moved toward fewer, deeper stories instead of high-volume wire coverage.

NYT’s daily publishing rate is remarkably stable, hovering around 6,000 articles a day regardless of month, dipping only modestly in July and around the winter holidays. February has fewer days. Of course it has fewer articles

3. Watching Words Rise and Fall

“Iraq” spikes hard in 2003, then flatlines over the following decade. “Obama” rises cleanly through two terms and drops off almost immediately after. “COVID” appears from nowhere in 2020, dominates, and fades.

 

And then “Trump”, whose line does something none of the others do: an initial surge in 2017, a slight dip, then an explosion upward through 2024 and 2025, ending at roughly 1,500 mentions per 10,000 articles. By a wide margin the single most concentrated presence of any word, person, or topic across the whole 25 years.

4. Finding What Made Each Era Different

  • Iraq blazes during the War on Terror years and fades to near-zero everywhere else. A word that mattered enormously for seven years, then largely vanished from the front page.

  • Obama appears only during his own era and disappears almost the moment he leaves office.

  • Trump is essentially absent before 2017, then dominates two consecutive eras.

  • Ukraine barely registers for twenty-two years, then suddenly becomes one of the defining terms of the post-COVID period.

And then one word stood apart from all of them.

  • China is the only term consistently present in every single era. Never disappearing. Never spiking dramatically. Just a steady undercurrent running through 25 years of American news. If you wanted one word for the most durable story of this century so far, that heatmap makes a quiet, compelling case.

5. What were they actually writing about?

    The results were part revealing, part deeply boring. The top two words were “paid” and “notice.” Not news. That’s the paid obituary section, which used consistent boilerplate headline language for two decades straight.

    Once I separated structural noise from real content, a clearer picture emerged: trump, china, obama, europe among the frequent proper nouns, alongside recurring topical words like business, court, health.

    Useful reminder for anyone doing text analysis on real-world data: raw word counts always surface the loudest, most repetitive patterns first, and those are usually administrative artifacts rather than signal. You have to actively hunt the noise before the interesting material becomes visible.

    Clinton appears throughout the dataset. But which one? Bill, president when the data begins in 2000? Or Hillary, prominent in her own right as Secretary of State and later a presidential candidate? A computer reading the bare word has no reliable way to know, especially in years when both were making headlines.

     

    Trump has the same problem in a different shape. Before 2015 the word overwhelmingly meant the brand: Trump Tower, Trump Hotels, Trump Organization deals. After 2015 it almost entirely means the man as a political figure.
